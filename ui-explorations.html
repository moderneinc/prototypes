WCAG contrast — primary color choices

Ratios computed with the WCAG 2.1 relative-luminance formula from the exact theme tokens each primary ships with (teal and green switch to their deep pair for light-mode text; blue uses Blue 700 for light-mode links). Blue is the recommended default — the best balance of color-vision-deficiency safety and contrast. The Blue AAA column is the mid-ramp token set (blue-400 dark / blue-800 light buttons and links) that reaches 7:1 everywhere — flip the app to it with the Blue AAA swatch.

Text (SC 1.4.3 / 1.4.6): AA ≥ 4.5:1 · AA Large ≥ 3:1 · AAA ≥ 7:1. UI components (SC 1.4.11): ≥ 3:1 against adjacent colors. Dark checks use the #100C0A panel; light checks use #FFFFFF.

Why this matters — selling to banks

Accessibility becomes a procurement requirement, not a preference. Banks will almost always ask for a VPAT (Voluntary Product Accessibility Template) or an ACR conforming to WCAG 2.1 AA, and often 2.2 AA now. Their vendor risk and procurement teams read it. A product with light-mode UI components failing the 3:1 contrast threshold, like the teal and green rows in this table, is a documented AA failure that shows up right there in the VPAT. That can stall or kill the deal in security review, which is the most expensive place to lose one.

Banks themselves are legally on the hook. US banks fall under ADA Title III, Section 508 if they touch government programs, and financial regulators expect accessible services. When they buy your software and put it in front of their customers or staff, your failures become their liability. So they contractually require you to meet the bar, and they audit it. “We’ll fix it later” doesn’t survive a bank’s third-party risk assessment.

The contract terms get sharp. Expect accessibility warranties, indemnification clauses, and the right to audit baked into the MSA. If you attest AA conformance and a row is actually failing, you’re not just losing a sale, you’re exposed on a signed representation.

The bar is higher than the general case. Banks tend to want AA across the board plus documented testing, keyboard-only operability, and screen reader support with tools like JAWS and NVDA. Contrast is the easy, visible part. They’ll also probe focus order, form error handling, and ARIA.

So with banks as your main customers, those two failing fills aren’t a UX rounding error. They’re a line item a procurement officer can point to and say “fix this before we sign.” The upside is unchanged: it’s a token-level fix, and clearing 3:1 turns a red row in your VPAT into a pass.

In order to support accessibility requirements per Section 508, Moderne UI will need to use the violet and blue primaries for all UI component boundaries and interactive elements, as both clear the WCAG 2.1 minimum of 3:1 for non-text contrast (SC 1.4.11), and avoid the teal and green fills in light mode, which fall short at 1.9:1 and 1.5:1.
